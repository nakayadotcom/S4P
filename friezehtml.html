11 Essential Sound Artworks, from Reviled Noise Orchestras to Protest-Minded Installations

By Tessa Solomon

In 1979, the Museum of Modern Art in New York presented a modest exhibition called "Sound Art." The museum's small media art gallery could only accommodate one artwork at a time, so the three works in the show, by Maggi Payne, Connie Beckley, and Julia Heyward, took turns on display.

In essence, London had helped define a medium that was newly emergent back then, but her explanation offered few parameters—which was exactly the point. Below, a look back at 11 essential sound artworks.

Luigi Russolo, Gran Concerto Futuristico (1917)

Luigi Russolo is best known as a painter associated with the Futurist movement, but he's also considered one of the first experimental noise artists.

Marcel Duchamp, Erratum Musical (1913)

Duchamp dedicated much of his time between 1912 and 1915 to musical composition. Erratum Musical was a score written for three voices and arranged by chance.

John Cage, 4'33" (1952)

4'33" is a three-movement composition consisting of four minutes and 33 seconds of silence, making spectators aware of ambient sounds.

Bill Fontana, Distant Trains (1984)

Live microphones in Köln Hauptbahnhof transmitted real-time acoustics, recreating a phantom sonic environment in Berlin.

Max Neuhaus, Times Square (1977–92)

Neuhaus's work features a pulsating drone that emanates 24/7 from a subway steam escape hatch in Times Square.

Carsten Nicolai, Reflektor Distortion (2016)

A rotating water basin was hit by low sound frequencies, making the invisible shape of sound visible to the human eye.

Jem Finer, Longplayer (1999)

A piece of ambient music that started on Dec 31, 1999, and will finish in the year 3000 using Tibetan singing bowls.
